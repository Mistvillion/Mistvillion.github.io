Hong Kong is a city you have to read slowly, one footstep at a time.

Its story is not written only in the dazzling skyline of Victoria Harbour. It hides among the crowded signboards of Mong Kok, inside the weathered old buildings of Yau Ma Tei, in the hurried crowds that flow along Queen's Road. It has the cold glare thrown back by glass curtain walls, and the warm, small, everyday bustle of the shops on its street corners; it has the sweeping view from Victoria Peak over the whole city, and the closeness of strangers brushing shoulders in narrow lanes.

I stayed in Hong Kong for three days. Three days is short, far too short to truly know a city this complicated, yet long enough for me to remember its light, its sounds, its smells and its weather. Those memories did not line up neatly by itinerary. They overlap: the neon of Mong Kok falls into the night, thick fog spills across the water of the harbour, Monet's light and shadow slowly merge in my mind with Wu Guanzhong's ink, and the wind on The Peak blows from the last afternoon of the trip all the way into the depths of memory.

When I think of Hong Kong now, the first thing that comes to mind is not any landmark building but the city's ever-changing face. It is sharp by day and soft at dusk; loud among crowds and silent in the sea breeze. It is like a fast-cut film, and also like a painting slowly blurred by mist.

## Day One: Between Mong Kok and Yau Ma Tei, Into the Hong Kong Night

On my first afternoon in Hong Kong, I went to Mong Kok. If a city has a heartbeat, Mong Kok is probably where Hong Kong's beats fastest.

The moment I stepped into the streets, sound came rushing in, layer upon layer. Double-decker buses crawled past, red taxis threaded through the junctions, and pedestrians gathered quickly as the lights changed, then scattered in every direction the instant the signal turned green. Music from the shops, the warning tones of vehicles, conversations on the pavement and the patter of footsteps blended into an urban rhythm that almost never stops. Overhead, signboards jutted thickly into the street, some bright and vivid, others worn by wind and rain past their original colours. They hung at uneven heights between the buildings, cutting an already narrow sky into irregular shapes.

![](旺角1.jpg)

Mong Kok's buildings make no effort to look refined. Their old facades carry the marks of time, window air conditioners line up beside cramped windows, and laundry sways gently between one building and the next. Down at street level, though, the energy never fades: convenience stores, pharmacies, restaurants and shops of every size stand shoulder to shoulder, people stream in and out of narrow doorways, and life fills every crack in the city like water. The crowding here is not just a sense of space; it is a kind of exuberant vitality. Hong Kong compresses countless people's daily lives onto a limited patch of land, so every floor, every street and every corner serves a different purpose. Some people live here, some work here, some are only passing through. Everyone's paths cross and recross, yet somehow they keep to a practised kind of order.

Walking on from Mong Kok towards Yau Ma Tei, the character of the city shifts almost without my noticing. There is no clear line between the two, but you can feel the streets growing quieter. Mong Kok is like a fire burning at its height, bright, dense and full of motion; Yau Ma Tei is more like a well-thumbed book, its pages yellowed and its corners curled, yet still holding a warmth you are reluctant to leave. The old buildings of Yau Ma Tei look even more weather-beaten. Faded walls, long narrow balconies and window frames marked by the years together make up the "old Hong Kong" so many people picture. But walking through it, you realise it is not a set preserved for nostalgia. It still belongs to living, everyday life: neighbours come out of shops carrying their bags, an old man crosses the junction slowly, a shop assistant stands by the door calling to customers, and the sound of a television drifts from an upstairs window.

The Hong Kong of the movies always carries an air of legend, while the real Yau Ma Tei is more fragmentary, and more moving. Those old street scenes, filmed by countless cameras, have not stayed in the past. They grow old while continuing to make room for new lives. The walls may be mottled, but the signs along the street still light up; new little shops open beside old-fashioned ones, and young people in school uniforms walk past elderly neighbours. The past and the present have not replaced each other. They are crowded onto the same street, breathing together.

After leaving Yau Ma Tei, I came to Victoria Harbour. Stepping out of the dense blocks, the view suddenly opened up. The pressure of the narrow streets fell away at once, replaced by water, sky and a wind blowing in from far off. The harbour is like a vast stretch of blank space on a scroll, gently parting the tightly packed towers on either shore, and giving this ever-upward-growing city, at last, somewhere to stretch out.

![](维多利亚港1.jpg)

Looking across the water to the far shore, skyscrapers spread out along the coastline. Sunlight fell on their glass curtain walls and bounced back cold and bright. Boats passed slowly, leaving long thin trails on the surface that the waves then quietly smoothed away. Standing by the water, I suddenly realised that Victoria Harbour is not just a famous sight for Hong Kong. It is more like the city taking a breath. The streets are too dense, the buildings too tall, the crowds too hurried; only at the water's edge can your gaze finally pass over everything in front of you and reach the distance. Buildings that seem towering, stern and unapproachable from inside the city become, seen from across the water, quiet outlines on the skyline.

Next, I went to The Hong Kong Polytechnic University. Its red-brick buildings offered a completely different kind of view. Compared with Mong Kok's crowded, almost organically grown streets, the campus felt rational, orderly and restrained. Its geometric buildings, interconnected walkways and clearly layered spaces were like a brief, steady pause amid the city's clamour. Yet the campus is not entirely cut off from Hong Kong. Traffic and towers still fill the distance, and the city's noise still drifts over the walls. The campus simply turns those sounds down for a while, offering a moment of quiet between the rushing of a journey.

![](PolyU.jpg)

After nightfall, I went back to Yau Ma Tei and Mong Kok. The same streets seemed to have undergone a silent transformation between day and night. The sky deepened, and the street lights came on one after another. Facades that had looked a little shabby by day withdrew into shadow, while the signs and shop windows became the true stars of the street. Red, blue, green and warm yellow light overlapped, falling on the faces of passers-by and shining on road surfaces polished by rain and wheels.

Night in Yau Ma Tei has an understated, cinematic quality. The lighting is never lavish, and many streets are actually quite dim. Windows in the old buildings glow here and there, the shops below spill warm light, and figures flicker past their doorways. When I looked up, the buildings stood silent in the dark, their countless windows seeming to hold countless lives of people who will never know one another. Night in Mong Kok burns more fiercely. The crowds had not thinned at all; if anything they were denser than by day. Lit-up buses rolled down the streets, shop signs hung high overhead, and the tide of people at the junctions kept gathering and dividing. The whole district was like a sea made of light, and the people moving through it were each no more than a fleeting shadow.

![](旺角2.jpg)

Hong Kong's most famous night view is usually tied to Victoria Harbour, but the night in Mong Kok and Yau Ma Tei is just as unforgettable. The harbour at night is grand, something to be gazed at from afar; the night in the streets is close at hand. Lights glow right against old buildings, signs hang over your head, cars brush past, and the hem of a stranger's coat sweeps across your line of sight. You are not standing far away looking at a night view. You have walked straight into it.

## Day Two: Light in the Museum, Fog on Victoria Harbour

On the afternoon of the second day, I went to the Hong Kong Museum of Art, where I saw works by Claude Monet and Wu Guanzhong.

Monet's paintings always make me think of time. What he paints seems to be not a fixed object, but the instant in which light falls upon it. Water, sky, the shadows of trees and the mist seep into one another, and clear boundaries are gently dissolved. Colour does not sit quietly on the canvas; it trembles softly in the viewer's eye. Up close, you can see the strokes the paint has left behind; step back a few paces, and those seemingly scattered patches of colour slowly gather into air, shimmering water and distance. The scenes do not seem to have been fully painted so much as to be slowly surfacing from a flowing light. Monet's world has no hard edges. Everything is changing: the morning fog will lift, the light on the water will dim, flowers will bloom with the season and then wither in the wind. What he painted is the trace that time leaves on the surface of the world.

Wu Guanzhong's work is another kind of flow. Black lines travel across the picture, now heavy, now light; broad expanses of white are like water, like mist, and like feelings left unspoken. The houses, branches, bridges and rivers of the Jiangnan region, once distilled, are no longer merely particular landscapes but rhythmic dots, lines and blocks of colour. His paintings carry both the spirit of Chinese ink painting and the structure of modern art. Among the blacks, whites and greys, a bright red, yellow or green occasionally leaps out, like a single note suddenly sounding in the silence. Within pictures that look simple lie rich layers, where sparse and dense, empty and full, light and heavy pull against one another.

If Monet captured how light makes the world hazy, Wu Guanzhong seemed to be studying how the fewest strokes of ink could summon the widest imagination. Seeing their work on the same afternoon was a strange and wonderful experience. One came from faraway France, chasing ever-vanishing moments through light and colour; the other set out from Chinese tradition, seeking a new path between ink and modern form. Their languages are different, yet both carry landscape beyond landscape itself. Water is not only water and mist is not only mist; they can become vessels for time, memory and feeling.

After the exhibition, I walked to a spot with a view of Victoria Harbour, and what I saw through the window stopped me in my tracks. That day the harbour had no bright sunshine and no crisp, sharp skyline. Thick fog was rising slowly off the water, and the moisture and wind brought by a typhoon had wrapped the entire harbour. The tops of the distant towers had vanished, leaving only faint, half-seen outlines; buildings further off had disappeared almost entirely, as though an invisible hand had gently erased them from the city.

![](维多利亚港2.jpg)

The sea took on a still, sombre grey, and the line between sky and water blurred. Now and then a boat emerged from the fog, crossed my field of view slowly, and faded away on the other side. Hong Kong's skyline, normally hard and upright, lost its edge in that moment and became soft and remote.

The scene before me overlapped strangely with the paintings I had just seen. It was as if the harbour had suddenly stepped into one of Monet's canvases. The light was crumbled by the mist, the buildings were reduced to hazy patches of colour, there was no clear boundary between near and far, and everything hovered between appearing and disappearing. It was like one of Wu Guanzhong's ink paintings, too. The dark buildings became scattered dots of ink, while sea and sky dissolved into wide stretches of grey-white blankness. The fog did not hide the scenery; it rearranged it. Some things were concealed, some outlines emphasised, and the parts that could not be seen left room for an even wider imagination.

The museum's glass kept people and weather apart for a while. Standing indoors, watching the fog creep bit by bit across Victoria Harbour, I suddenly felt that reality and painting were not so far apart after all. Painters turn the world they see into pictures, and the weather, too, was painting outside the window. The wind was an invisible brush, the fog a pigment endlessly spreading, and the sea a vast and silent canvas. Hong Kong's iconic buildings flickered in and out of this painting, becoming an unstable presence.

Many people come to Victoria Harbour hoping to see the classic Hong Kong: clear skies, sharply defined towers, and after dark, the lights of countless homes stretching along the shore. What I saw instead was a Hong Kong hidden in fog. It was not complete, not even quite "standard", and for that very reason it felt closer to an encounter that can never be repeated. Someone photographs the harbour on a clear day every single day, but the fog of that afternoon belonged only to that gust of wind, that bank of cloud, and that moment of the sea.

What is truly precious about travel is often not finally seeing a sight you have long awaited, but meeting, outside of any plan, another face the world happens to reveal.

![](维多利亚港3.jpg)

The Hong Kong of the second day was no longer only bustle and splendour. It became quiet, damp and reserved, like a sentence left unfinished, or a letter soaked through by mist.

## Day Three: Through the Sharp Light of Central, Up to Victoria Peak

On the morning of the third day, I crossed to the other side of Victoria Harbour, to Central. Riding the Ferris wheel, I took in both shores at a glance, and suddenly felt that old poet's sense of standing on the summit, with every other mountain small below.

![](皇后大道2.jpg)

Heading deeper into Central, I came to Queen's Road, the heart of Hong Kong in quite another sense.

If Mong Kok and Yau Ma Tei show the city's life close to the ground, Central reveals Hong Kong's ambition to grow upwards. Office blocks, banks, hotels and malls are packed tightly into limited space, and enormous glass curtain walls cut the sky into bright fragments while reflecting the surrounding buildings layer upon layer.

![](皇后大道1.jpg)

Tall buildings blot out part of the sky and their shadows cover the pavement, while the glass facades on the other side shine almost blindingly. Walking between the towers, a person seems especially small. The crowds on Queen's Road are different from Mong Kok's. The steps here are just as quick, but less noisy and more restrained. Smartly dressed people cross the junctions holding coffee, documents or phones, and soon vanish into office entrances. Traffic flows along the roads, and tram tracks, footbridges, slopes and streets weave together into a complex and efficient urban system.

Hong Kong is a three-dimensional city. Its roads exist not only on the ground but also in mid-air. Footbridges connect office towers and malls, escalators carry people from one level to the next, and stairs and ramps keep turning between buildings. Sometimes, after walking for a long while, you look back and realise you left the original street long ago and are now suspended above the traffic and the towers. This sense of depth comes partly from the terrain and partly from Hong Kong's almost extreme use of space. Mountains and sea limit the city's boundaries, so people build upwards, stacking roads, commerce, transport and daily life layer upon layer. Hong Kong is not spread out across the land; it seems to have grown up out of a narrow strip of coastline.

Then I walked into IFC Mall. Inside, it was bright and spotless, with a sense of order completely different from the streets outside. Glass, metal and pale walls together formed a calm space, the light fell evenly, and the damp outdoor air and noisy traffic were shut out. Yet the huge floor-to-ceiling windows brought the city back into view. Through the glass you could see the harbour, the roads and tier upon tier of buildings. Indoors, people drank coffee, shopped and chatted; outside the window, Hong Kong kept on running. Here, a mall is not just a self-contained space for shopping. It is more like part of the city's transport network, linked to the MTR, office towers and footbridges. You can pass from one building into another, through malls and corridors, and arrive in an entirely different district without ever having to return to street level.

Hong Kong has turned movement itself into city life.

In the afternoon, I went up to Victoria Peak. The ascent was like slowly folding up the streets I had walked through that day. As we climbed, the nearby buildings gradually sank beneath my feet, and the harbour, once hidden by buildings, unfolded bit by bit. The skyscrapers that had seemed so huge and oppressive at ground level now shrank into dense geometric shapes.

Standing on The Peak and looking down over Hong Kong, I could at last see the city's full geographic outline. The towers of Hong Kong Island grow tight against the foot of the mountain, Victoria Harbour spreads out through the middle of the city, and Kowloon stretches away on the other side of the water. The buildings were like a forest of steel, concrete and glass, while the sea left a dark gap between the two shores. Boats moved slowly through the harbour, so small from up high that they looked like white dots scattered across the water.

The wind on The Peak is more direct than in the city. It came over the trees and railings and met me head-on, carrying the moist breath of the sea. The fog had still not fully lifted, and the distant buildings were veiled in a faint grey-white. The city's edges were no longer clear, and mountain, sea, sky and towers gradually merged into one along my line of sight.

![](太平山顶1.jpg)

Looking at Hong Kong from Victoria Peak produces a strange illusion. When you walk at ground level, the city feels so vast it seems to have no end. One street follows another, buildings hide buildings, crowds pour in from every direction, and it is hard to find any real boundary. But standing up high, those bewildering streets suddenly took on an order, and the skyscrapers fell quiet.

The crowding, the din and the hurry I had felt while inside the city were all gently smoothed away by distance. Central was no longer only a symbol of finance and commerce, and Mong Kok no longer only a blaze of neon. Together they made up the complicated city beneath my feet, clinging close to each other between the mountains and the sea.

Perhaps looking down on a city always stirs a kind of tenderness. Distance lets us forget its sharp edges for a while, so we see only the whole formed by lights, coastline and buildings. The people living alone behind countless windows, the joys and sorrows that unfold in its streets and alleys, the stories that begin and end in haste, are all gathered into the same view.

![](太平山顶2.jpg)

Standing on Victoria Peak, I looked back over the Hong Kong I had walked through in three days.

On the first day, I felt its warmth among the crowds of Mong Kok and Yau Ma Tei; on the second, through the museum's glass, I watched it grow soft in thick fog; on the third, I finally stood up high and saw how this city leans against the mountains and faces the sea.

The three days were also three ways of looking at Hong Kong. On the first day, I walked into its streets. On the second, I gazed at it through art and weather. On the third, I stood far off and tried to see it whole.

## After Leaving, Hong Kong Still Glows in Memory

Once a journey is over, many of the specific routes may slowly blur. What truly stays are often moments that cannot be precisely marked on any map.

I will remember the layers of signboards over Mong Kok's streets. They block out the sky, yet they light up the street. I will remember the windows of Yau Ma Tei's old buildings. By day they look ordinary, even shabby; at night, every lit window is like a story not yet finished. I will remember the sea breeze of Victoria Harbour, and the way the view suddenly opened as I stood by the water. The city behind me was still loud, but the water before me carried every sound away into the distance. I will also remember the light and shadow on Queen's Road, sliced up by tall buildings, and the bright, restrained glass spaces inside IFC Mall.

But of course, what I will remember most is the typhoon I stumbled upon so unexpectedly on that second afternoon.

That fog made the familiar harbour strange, and briefly linked the paintings in the museum with the reality outside the window. The flowing light of Monet's canvases and the vast blank spaces of Wu Guanzhong's ink both found an answer on that grey sea. The fog hid part of Hong Kong, yet let me see another part of it. This city usually presents itself through speed, efficiency and prosperity. Its towers are sharply outlined, its night views dazzling, its streets forever thronged with people. Yet when thick fog descends and the edges of its buildings are erased little by little, Hong Kong reveals a soft, hazy, even faintly melancholy side.

Three days are, of course, not enough to read all of Hong Kong. But the Hong Kong of those three days will remain forever in a moment woven of thick fog, sea wind and neon: damp, bright, crowded, gorgeous, and touched with a sorrow that can never quite be put into words.
