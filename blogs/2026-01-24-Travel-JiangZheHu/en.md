As the old saying goes, "Above there is heaven; below there are Suzhou and Hangzhou." Taking advantage of the winter break and the off-season, a few friends and I set out for the Yangtze River Delta to see the densely packed skyline of Shanghai, the winding paths of the Suzhou gardens, and the vast misty waters of West Lake in Hangzhou. We left Guangzhou Baiyun Airport on January 17 and landed at Shanghai Pudong International Airport in the early hours of the 18th.

## First Stop: Shanghai Jiao Tong University (Minhang Campus)

Our first stop was Shanghai Jiao Tong University. SJTU began as Nanyang Public School, founded in Shanghai by Sheng Xuanhuai in 1896, and it hardly needs an introduction from me. As it happens, one of my friends studies there, so we had him show us around this world-famous university.

![](上海交通大学1.jpg)

A wonderfully creative signpost: with Shanghai as the starting point, London and Stockholm lie to the west; Hong Kong, Taipei and Sydney to the south; San Francisco to the east; and Beijing to the north.

![](./上海交通大学2.jpg)

The Pao Yue-Kong Library on the shore of Siyuan Lake was named after Sir Pao Yue-Kong, the Hong Kong shipping magnate, after his death in 1992, in memory of his donations to education.

![](上海交通大学3.jpg)

The campus also has a botanical garden, the Tsung-Dao Lee Library, an illustration gallery, and the School of Electronic Information and Electrical Engineering, which had just been split up.

In winter SJTU looks a little bare, with the trees along the roads stripped of their leaves, but that didn't change how much I wanted to be there.

The university motto: **When you drink water, remember its source; love the nation and bring honour to the school.**

We had lunch at a Western restaurant on campus, which was a bit pricey. Dinner was teppanyaki at Longhu New Street near the university, which was pricier still. Food and drink alone cost us nearly 200 yuan that day.

The dream school I didn't get into in the 2024 gaokao.

## Second Stop: Fudan University (Handan and Jiangwan Campuses)

Next we went to Fudan University, another of Shanghai's top universities. Fudan was founded in 1905, and its name comes from a line in the Shangshu Dazhuan: "the sun and moon shine, dawn after dawn". It was drizzling that day, so the photos may not look their best, but that didn't lessen my respect for Fudan. Once again, a friend of ours studies there, so we asked him to be our guide.

![](复旦大学1.jpg)

The Handan Campus feels as if it has been "chopped up" by the public roads running through it, and it is very spread out. A single campus is divided into a South, North and East area. Our friend told us the North canteen has the best food and the East canteen the best value, so we had him take us to the North canteen, and it really was good. After the day before's spending spree, an 18-yuan plate of beef with scrambled egg on rice felt very welcome.

Guanghua Tower, completed for Fudan's centenary in 2005, whose name also comes from "the sun and moon shine":

![](复旦大学2.jpg)

The Jiangwan Campus is newer, and apart from the Law School it is almost entirely graduate students. I have to say the new campus looks grand, with one architectural style throughout and well-planned roads that feel clean and open.

The KoGuan Law Building. Where Guanghua Tower aims for grand symbolism, the law building's design leans towards solemnity, reflecting the rigorous, pragmatic ethos of the Law School.

![](复旦大学3.jpg)

As we walked, our friend told us about Shanghai's metro station names and the "Greater Shanghai Plan", drawn up by the Nationalist government in the late 1920s and 1930s to build a new city centre at Jiangwan, outside the foreign concessions. On the metro ride to Fudan, you notice that many station names use the characters "Min" (people), "Guo" (nation) and "Zheng" (government), or are named after cities, such as Guoquan Road and Nanjing South Road. These road names are what the plan left behind. The heart of the plan was the civic centre district, home to a group of buildings in the Chinese Classical Revival style designed under the architect Dong Dayou. They are built on reinforced concrete frames yet topped with double-eaved hip-and-gable roofs, embodying the spirit of "national self-strengthening". The old Shanghai City Hall from that group still stands: it is the Green-Tiled Building on the campus of Shanghai University of Sport.

## Third Stop: The Bund and Yu Garden

On the way back to the hotel from Fudan, we stopped by the Bund. I had been here once before, on a high school study trip on April 5, 2021, when a foreign visitor came up and asked to take a photo with me. I still remember it clearly. Nearly five years later, Shanghai's busiest stretch was as grand and dazzling as ever. On one side stand the old riverside buildings in many different styles, known as the "exhibition of world architecture". On the other side, across the Huangpu River, are the Oriental Pearl Tower and the skyscrapers of Lujiazui.

Let me lose myself in a dream of luxury...

![](上海外滩.jpg)

A ten-odd minute walk brought us to Yu Garden. It was built in the Ming dynasty by Pan Yunduan as a private garden where his parents could enjoy their old age, and "Yu" means "to please one's elderly parents". Today it is a classic Jiangnan garden that stands in sharp contrast to modern Lujiazui. It is both a quiet classical garden and a busy cultural and shopping district.

![](豫园.jpg)

## Fourth Stop: Suzhou Museum and the Humble Administrator's Garden

Leaving Shanghai, we arrived in Suzhou, a city like a double-sided embroidery that weaves "ancient charm" and "modern style" together.

I had long heard of the Suzhou gardens, and today we visited the elder brother of all Jiangnan gardens: the Humble Administrator's Garden. Since Suzhou Museum is right next door, we went to the museum first.

Suzhou Museum was designed by the master architect I. M. Pei, and it combines the white walls and dark tiles of Jiangnan gardens with geometric lines and modern light. The new building opened in 2006, and Pei called it his "youngest daughter". Inside are many treasures, including the sword of Fuchai, King of Wu, ceramics, paintings and calligraphy by scholars, and all sorts of curious rocks and antiquities.

The museum has even set up a few spots made for photos:

![](苏州博物馆1.jpg)

A translucent screen hangs in front of a window, so that photographing the scenery outside through it gives the feel of an ink-wash painting.

Suzhou Museum photographed through the screen:

![](苏州博物馆2.jpg)

Leaving through the museum's exit, we came to the residence of Li Xiucheng, the Loyal Prince of the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom (Prince Zhong's Mansion), which displays the history of the Taiping Rebellion.

After that we went next door to the most celebrated of all the Suzhou gardens, the Humble Administrator's Garden.

Its name comes from the line "this, too, is the governance of a humble man" in Pan Yue's "Rhapsody on Living in Idleness" from the Western Jin dynasty. The garden was begun in the Zhengde era of the Ming dynasty by Wang Xianchen, who came home to build it after his official career stalled. The famous painter Wen Zhengming took part in its design. The whole garden is centred on water, with pavilions, terraces and halls built along its edges, and the view changes with every few steps.

Sadly, in winter the trees had shed all their leaves and the garden looked a little desolate. The upside was that there were fewer people, which made it easier to take in the mood of the garden itself.

Looking towards the North Temple Pagoda from the Yihong Pavilion. This is a classic example of "borrowed scenery": a pagoda outside the walls is pulled into the garden's view.

## Fifth Stop: Hanshan Temple and the Lingering Garden

"Beyond the walls of Gusu lies Hanshan Temple; its midnight bell reaches the traveller's boat." Zhang Ji's poem "A Night Mooring by Maple Bridge" has beaten in time with the cultural pulse of Suzhou for over a thousand years. Hanshan Temple was first built in the Liang dynasty of the Southern Dynasties, and is said to be named after Hanshan, a Tang dynasty monk who once lived there.

![](寒山寺.jpg)

The Lingering Garden. Together with the Humble Administrator's Garden it is known as one of the "twin jewels" of the Suzhou gardens, and it is also one of the "Four Great Gardens of China", the other two being the Summer Palace in Beijing and the Chengde Mountain Resort. Its Cloud-Capped Peak is a famous Taihu stone.

![](留园.jpg)

## Sixth Stop: West Lake

After a few days in Suzhou, we moved on to Hangzhou to take in the scenery of West Lake. West Lake became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2011, and the Su Causeway, the Bai Causeway and the Broken Bridge are all on the lake.

As Zhang Dai wrote in "Viewing Snow from the Lake Pavilion": "Rime and mist hung thick; sky, clouds, hills and water were one white, above and below."

In winter, West Lake is less crowded and noisy, and much calmer.

## Epilogue

Set out on January 17, returned on the 23rd, finished this blog on the 24th.

Shanghai: **Pearl-shod crowds come and go, every scene a wonder; screens shift and change, each one a splendid page.**

Suzhou: **Why seek the heart's delight in distant fields? A nearby garden plainly holds faraway feeling.**

Hangzhou: **The shimmering water is lovely in sunshine; the hazy hills are wondrous in the rain.**

Just like the Hangzhou that Liu Yong painted in "Watching the Tide": misty willows and painted bridges, wind-stirred curtains and emerald screens, a hundred thousand homes rising unevenly.
